From Fences to Freedom: Mastering Boundaries for Spiritual Victory

This episode explores the vital role of boundaries in protecting your spiritual recovery and relationships. You'll discover how to build godly 'hedges' that guard your heart while fostering healthy connections, differentiate between walls and boundaries, and implement practical strategies for setting limits in your inner circle, grey-area relationships, and opposite-gender interactions. Walk away with actionable steps to create a secure, flourishing life in Christ.

================================================================

In my book Only Faith, Book 3, Shoes of the Gospel of Peace, we turn to Chapter 23—Setting Boundaries. This chapter isn’t about building walls to push people away. It’s about creating fences with gates that let the right things in while keeping destructive influences out. Think of your heart like a garden. You’ve spent weeks clearing the rocks, planting good seeds, and nurturing healthy growth. But if you leave that garden unprotected, the wild animals of temptation will trample everything you’ve built. Boundaries are the hedges that guard your recovery, your relationships, and your identity in Christ. Now, I know the word *boundary* can feel uncomfortable. We’ve been taught that being a Christian means saying yes to everyone, all the time. But here’s the truth: refusing to set limits isn’t loving—it’s lazy. It’s letting others run the show of your life instead of taking responsibility for the temple God gave you. In this episode, we’re diving into why boundaries matter, how they’re different from walls, and the three key areas where you need to build your hedges right now. We’ll talk about protecting your closest relationships, navigating tricky grey-area friendships, and setting non-negotiable lines with opposite-gender interactions. You’ll learn how to turn the ‘no’ that feels selfish into a ‘no’ that actually sets you free. And yes, we’ll address the hard questions: What if people push back? What if your boundaries feel lonely? What if you’re afraid you’re doing this wrong? I’ve been there. But here’s the good news—when you guard your heart with purpose, you’re not just protecting yourself. You’re creating space for God to do His best work. So let’s get practical. Let’s build fences that let the light in and keep the darkness out. So, what’s the difference between a wall and a boundary? A wall is like building a concrete barrier when you’re hurting or ashamed—you’re trying to shut everything out, but it ends up cutting you off from healing, from real connection. You’re physically there but emotionally closed off, and that deadness just keeps you stuck. Boundaries, though? They’re like a fence with a gate. You’re still open to people you trust, to your spouse, your accountability partners, your church family. But you’re smart about what you let in. See, a boundary isn’t about hiding—it’s about creating a safe space where you can actually be vulnerable without fear. It’s like an operating room for your heart, where the Holy Spirit can do His deep work without distractions or triggers. And here’s the thing: God is the original boundary-setter. He didn’t just throw the world together willy-nilly—He set limits, like telling the oceans where to stop and giving us His commands to protect us. His rules aren’t about control; they’re about keeping us safe so we can thrive. When you set boundaries, you’re not being selfish—you’re being a good steward of your heart, your mind, your body. You’re saying, ‘I value my purity, my relationships, and what God is doing in me enough to protect them.’ Now, let’s be real—people might push back when you start setting these limits. Old friends, enablers, even well-meaning folks might get defensive or guilt-trip you. But that pushback is a good sign! It means your boundary is actually working. Those people were used to you being an open door for their chaos, and now you’re saying, ‘No more.’ The key is to stay firm but kind. Boundaries aren’t walls—they’re fences that let the right people in while keeping the wrong stuff out. And when you do this, you create room for real connection, real growth, and real freedom. It’s not about isolation; it’s about building a life where your heart is safe, your relationships are healthy, and you can finally stop running from your past. So start small. Pick one area where you need a fence, talk to someone you trust, and let God help you guard what matters most. So, where do you actually start building these fences? Usually, they fall into three main areas. First, your inner circle—your spouse and accountability partners. These are the people closest to you, so boundaries here create a culture of trust. Maybe you decide no phones in the bedroom at night or give your accountability partner full access to your browser history. These aren’t punishments; they’re safeguards. If you’re married, these boundaries show your spouse you’re serious about change. You’re saying, ‘I value your heart more than my privacy.’ Next, grey-area relationships—coworkers, old friends, or people who drain your energy. They’re not bad, but they make purity harder. Maybe a coworker always brings up crude topics, or an old friend hangs out in risky places. A boundary here could mean skipping that lunch spot with the team or limiting time with that person to thirty minutes a week. You’re distancing yourself from enablers who don’t respect your new direction. Finally, opposite-gender interactions. This is non-negotiable. Protect your marriage by setting radical limits: no meetings alone, no secret texting, no emotional intimacy that belongs with your spouse or accountability partner. Keep communication professional and transparent. These boundaries aren’t about being legalistic—they’re about being wise. The enemy exploits weak spots, so if a connection lacks clear limits, he’ll slip in. Think of boundaries as fences with gates, not walls. They keep the right people in while filtering out what harms you. Walls isolate; boundaries connect by creating safety. For example, telling your spouse, ‘I won’t use my laptop after 10 pm,’ is specific and actionable. It’s not about control—it’s about stewardship. You’re guarding the temple of your heart. When you enforce these hedges, you’re not shutting people out; you’re creating space for healing and growth. It’s uncomfortable at first, but it’s the only way to build a life where your new identity in Christ can thrive. Here’s the deal: if you set a boundary but don’t follow through with a consequence, you’re just asking nicely. The enemy knows this. Think of it like telling someone not to walk into your garden, but when they trample your plants, you just sigh and let them go. They’ll keep coming back. Boundaries need teeth—real actions you’re ready to take if someone crosses the line. Maybe it’s deleting a contact, limiting time with a friend, or cutting off communication with someone who keeps tempting you. It’s not about being mean; it’s about protecting your heart and your freedom. You have to be clear about what happens if the boundary is broken. For example, if a coworker keeps making inappropriate jokes, you might say, ‘If you bring that up again, I’ll have to end the conversation.’ Then you actually do it. That’s how you build respect for your limits. Now, when people push back—especially the ones who thrived on your old habits—they’ll use guilt trips, mock you, or say you’ve gone too far. That’s normal. Their pushback means your boundary is actually working. You’re not the easy target anymore. Even ‘good’ people might trigger you because of past stuff or because they don’t get your new way of living. If being around them strains your resolve, step back. Your purity is worth more than trying to be everyone’s friend. And don’t feel bad about distancing from someone just because they’re ‘spiritual.’ If their presence leads you back into old patterns, walk away. It’s not about being judgmental; it’s about being wise. Remember, you’re in a war. Your job is to guard the gates of your heart. If someone won’t respect your limits, you might have to build a wall for a while. That’s okay. Your freedom and your future are worth it. As Proverbs 4:23 says, ‘Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it spring the issues of life.’ That’s your building permit for every fence you put up. So be brave. Your boundaries are fences of freedom, not walls of shame. The uncomfortable truth is, sometimes you have to say ‘no’ to stay ‘yes’ to the life God has for you. So here’s what you do this week, friend. First, take a hard look at where your relational fence is most broken—maybe it’s a specific person, a time of day when you’re vulnerable, or an app that’s a constant temptation. Name it clearly, like ‘I’m avoiding my old friend who makes me feel guilty for setting limits’ or ‘I can’t use my phone in bed anymore.’ Next, write down three solid boundaries that actually protect you. Don’t say vague stuff like ‘I’ll try to be better.’ Be specific: ‘I won’t check my phone after 10 pm,’ or ‘I’ll share my browser history with my accountability partner every Sunday.’ These are your guardrails. Then, pick one person you trust and tell them a boundary. You don’t need to explain everything—just say, ‘To protect my healing, I’m not going to anymore. I’d really appreciate your support.’ Keep it simple. Oh, and memorize Proverbs 4:23 this week. ‘Keep your heart with all diligence’ isn’t just a Bible verse—it’s your permission slip to build those fences. Think of it like a blueprint: it gives you the right to say ‘no’ to anything that threatens your heart. And if you feel guilty about setting limits? Remember, God sets boundaries too. He created the world with lines and rules, not just to control us, but to keep us safe. Your boundaries aren’t walls to hide behind—they’re fences with gates that let in the right people and keep the wrong ones out. So take these steps, even if they feel uncomfortable. Your heart is worth it. And when you start living with those hedges up, you’ll feel the freedom shift from ‘I’m trying not to mess up’ to ‘I’m building something good that needs protection.’ That’s the kind of freedom Christ offers. You’ve got this.
