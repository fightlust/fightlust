From Legal Eviction to Sovereign Occupation: Enforcing the Victory of the Cross

This episode explores the Renouncing Protocol—a three-step legal eviction process to break spiritual agreements with the enemy—and the shift from managing chains to ruling your heart with God’s authority. Learn how to repent, renounce, and receive with vocal authority, handle residual guilt, and reclaim your Kingdom rank as a sovereign warrior in Christ.

================================================================

In my book fightlust, Book 2, The Breastplate of Righteousness, we turn to Chapter 13 — The Renouncing Protocol. This is where we roll up our sleeves and get tactical about breaking the legal agreements you’ve unknowingly signed with the enemy. Think of it as a courtroom drama in your soul: you’re not just fighting temptation anymore, you’re serving an eviction notice to a defeated opponent. The core idea? Every chain you carry — every addiction, every lie, every pattern that feels like your ‘normal’ — started with a choice. A choice to believe a lie about God, to accept a label the enemy slung at you, or to trade His relief for a quick fix. But here’s the good news: you don’t have to live under those contracts anymore. In this chapter, I walk you through the ‘Repent, Renounce, Receive’ framework — a three-step legal eviction process that flips the script. First, you own your part in the agreement without drowning in shame. Then, you stand on your feet and speak loud enough for the enemy to hear: ‘I renounce this lie! I evict you in Jesus’ name!’ Finally, you flood that empty space with the Holy Spirit’s truth so the darkness can’t sneak back in. And here’s the non-negotiable: you have to say it out loud. Your voice isn’t just a tool — it’s a weapon. Why? Because in the spiritual realm, words are binding. When you speak, you’re not just thinking; you’re signing a new legal document. Your brain, the enemy, and even your body all register the authority of your voice. So today, we’re diving into how to tear up those old contracts, enforce the victory of the cross, and start living like the free person God always meant you to be. Let’s get to work. Here's the deal with Colossians 2:14-15—it’s the ultimate legal briefing for your spiritual freedom. Let me break it down. First, Jesus wiped out your debt. The ‘handwriting of requirements’? That’s the list of your sins, the legal document the enemy used to claim you. But Christ nailed it to the cross. Your debt is paid in full. No more liens on your soul. Second, the enemy is disarmed. At the cross, Jesus stripped him of all power. He doesn’t have weapons anymore—except the ones you give him by agreeing with his lies. And third, the victory is public. The resurrection was a parade where Jesus declared, ‘I win!’ in front of every angel and demon. You’re not fighting a powerful foe—you’re evicting a defeated squatter. This isn’t about negotiating terms or begging for mercy. It’s about enforcing a court order. Your freedom isn’t based on your performance or how ‘good’ you are. It’s based on what Jesus already did. When you stand in that truth, you’re not struggling alone. You’re a bailiff of the King, serving an eviction notice. The enemy has *zero* legal right to your mind, your body, or your future. You don’t need to beg him to leave. You can command him out in Jesus’ name because the case is already closed. The debt is gone. The enemy is powerless. The victory is yours. And that’s not just a spiritual truth—it’s your daily reality. So when guilt or shame tries to creep in, remember: you’re not a tenant in a broken house. You’re the owner of a palace. The King already paid the rent. Your job is to walk in that freedom, loud and proud. So here’s the deal: breaking chains is like a one-time battle, but occupying is about daily leadership. When you first shatter a stronghold, it’s intense work—like smashing locks with a sledgehammer. That’s necessary, but it’s just the start. The real freedom comes when you shift from fighting to ruling. Imagine you’re not just defending a hill you’ve taken in war, but building a palace on it. That’s occupying. You’re not just ‘not a slave’ anymore; you’re a commissioned officer of the light, running your heart and mind like a Kingdom province. It’s moving from panic to peace, from gasping for air to breathing freely. You stop seeing truth as a weapon you grab in a crisis and start living in it like oxygen. And here’s the kicker: residual guilt? That’s just emotional debris. The enemy whispers, ‘You still feel bad, so you’re still dirty,’ but you gotta shut that down. Say, ‘I’m clean because the King says so!’ You’re not a tenant of the dark anymore—you’re the owner. Your rank? Ephesians 2:6 says you’re already seated in heavenly places with Christ. No more fugitive mindset, no more hiding. You’re promoted from the pit to the palace. So stop fighting every day and start leading. Your freedom isn’t just about survival—it’s about significance. You’re not just breaking chains; you’re building a legacy. Even after you break a chain, your soul might still feel the ghost-pressure of the past—like an old scar that twinges when the weather changes. The enemy uses this to whisper, 'See? You still feel dirty. You’re not really free.' But here’s the truth: that ache isn’t proof you’re still bound. It’s just the leftover static from a dead contract, like the echo of a song that’s already stopped playing. You can’t erase every memory or feeling overnight, but you can refuse to let them rent space in your mind. When the guilt hits, don’t argue with it or pretend it’s not there. Instead, speak legal finality over it. Say, 'That’s just atmospheric static from a dead contract! I’ve been washed by the blood of Jesus, and I’m not entertaining this ghost anymore.' Declare it loud enough that your own ears hear it. You’re not pretending to be clean—you’re reminding yourself of the reality. The King already said you’re clean, so the enemy’s accusations are just noise. Every time you choose to believe His verdict over your feelings, you’re clearing out the debris faster. It’s like sweeping up broken glass: you don’t have to love the mess, but you do have to act like the owner who’s reclaiming the room. The more you declare, 'I’m clean because the King says I’m clean,' the more you prove to yourself—and to the enemy—that you’re not a tenant in your own soul anymore. You’re the rightful owner, and your heart is a palace, not a prison. Keep doing this, and soon the ghost-pressure will fade, not because you forgot the past, but because you’ve made a new home in the present. Here's the thing: when you're stuck in a cycle of addiction or bondage, it's not just about bad habits—it's about your identity. You start feeling like a nobody, like you're hiding in the shadows, waiting for the next failure to catch up with you. But Ephesians 2:6 drops a bombshell: you’re not some lowly private in the enemy’s army. You’re already seated with Christ in the heavenly places. That’s not some far-off promise—it’s your reality right now. The problem is, when addiction takes hold, it steals your sense of authority. You start walking through life like a fugitive, always looking over your shoulder, thinking you’re one mistake away from being exposed. But what if you flipped that? What if you started acting like the general you are? Imagine carrying yourself like you belong in the palace, not the dungeon. That’s what reclaiming your Kingdom rank is all about. It’s not just about stopping bad behavior—it’s about stepping into the power and purpose God gave you. You’re not here to survive; you’re here to lead. When you walk in that truth, you stop reacting to triggers and start making decisions that line up with who you really are. You stop asking, ‘Will I mess up today?’ and start declaring, ‘I lead with the authority of Christ.’ It’s like upgrading from a broken, temporary tent to a permanent palace. Your heart isn’t a battlefield to defend—it’s a kingdom to rule. And the more you live like the sovereign governor of your soul, the less space there is for the enemy to wiggle in. You’re not just free from chains; you’re built for significance. So stop playing small. You’re a general, not a prisoner—now act like it.
