From Fall to Fortress: The rise Protocol for Spiritual Resilience

This episode breaks down the rise protocol (Repent, Investigate, Speak, Engage) for overcoming setbacks in spiritual warfare. We explore how to cut shame spirals, identify tactical gaps, lean on accountability, and rebuild stronger boundaries. The discussion moves beyond surface-level recovery to address emotional healing, grace, and the enemy’s ambush tactics after a stumble.

================================================================

In my book fightlust, Book 4, The Shield of Faith, we turn to Chapter 23 — The Rise Protocol — where I lay out a battle-tested plan for when you fall short. Let me be clear: This isn’t about perfection. It’s about resilience. You’ve stumbled. The enemy pounces, whispering lies like, *‘You’re a hypocrite now. God’s disgusted. You’ll never recover.’* But here’s the truth — the first step isn’t waiting for your shame to fade or your emotions to settle. It’s *acting*. Right now. The rise protocol — Repent, Investigate, Speak, Engage — is your emergency response to keep the enemy from turning a single failure into a full-blown collapse. Repent isn’t about guilt-tripping yourself; it’s a spiritual stop-loss. You run straight to the Father, not when you ‘feel holy’ again, but *when the fall happens*. Then you dig into the details: What triggered you? Were you halt — hungry, angry, lonely, tired? Where was the boundary breach? This isn’t self-punishment — it’s intelligence gathering. Next, you speak to your accountability partner, not hiding in shame but bringing the mess into the light. And finally, you rebuild — physically removing triggers, deleting apps, scheduling rest — so the enemy can’t use the same door again. But here’s the hard part: The shame spiral. Even after you’ve done all the steps, that voice in your head says, *‘You’re a failure. This proves you’re broken.’* That’s not God — that’s the enemy’s last ambush. I’ll show you how to drown it out with grace, how to walk in the mercy of the Prodigal Son’s father, and why your identity isn’t tied to your mistakes. This isn’t just about recovery — it’s about becoming someone the enemy can’t keep down. Let’s get into it. Here's the deal: when you mess up, the enemy doesn't want you to act fast. He wants you to wait until you 'feel better' or 'ready' to fix things. But that's exactly what he wants you to do. Think of it like a soldier on the battlefield—when you get hit, you don’t sit there and wait for the pain to go away. You grab your first-aid kit and start treating the wound right away. If you wait too long, that injury gets infected, and now you’ve got a bigger problem. Same with your spiritual health. If you delay repenting, investigating, confessing, and rebuilding your boundaries, you’re basically giving the enemy time to dig new trenches and reinforce his attack. The longer you wait, the stronger those neural pathways of failure get. Your brain starts thinking, 'Oh, this is normal. This is okay.' And suddenly, what was a one-time slip becomes a habit. But if you hit the rise protocol hard and fast—like, within minutes—you’re cutting off the enemy’s supply line. You’re not trying to be perfect; you’re just moving faster than he expects. You don’t need to 'earn' your way back into grace. You just need to act like a warrior who knows the war’s already won. So when the stumble happens, drop everything. Repent. Figure out what went wrong. Tell your partner. Then rebuild your defenses stronger than before. Because here’s the truth: the enemy’s plan only works if you let it. The faster you rise, the less power his attack has. And the more you prove to yourself—and to him—that you’re not defined by the fall. You’re defined by how you get back up. Here’s the thing—after you’ve done all the steps in rise, you might still feel like a storm cloud hanging over you. You repented, talked to your partner, fixed the gaps, but your heart’s still heavy. That’s because the real battle isn’t just about what you did—it’s about what the enemy is trying to make you *believe* about yourself. Grace isn’t just a safety net to catch you when you fall; it’s the solid ground you stand on to rise. Think about the Prodigal Son: when he came home, he didn’t get a lecture or a punishment. His dad threw a party, gave him a robe, and said, ‘You’re still my son.’ That’s mercy in action. But here’s the twist: grace isn’t a license to keep messing up. It’s the fuel that makes you *want* to change because you realize how much the Father loves you. The shame spiral? It’s the enemy’s way of making you feel like a failure, like your mistakes define you. But God’s grace says, ‘No, your identity is in Me. Your sin is covered, and your future is still bright.’ When you walk in that kind of freedom, you stop punishing yourself and start protecting your heart. You’re not perfect, but you’re *loved*. And that’s the only platform strong enough to keep you from drowning in your past. So breathe. You’re not stuck in the mess—you’re standing on the promise. Grace isn’t about ignoring the problem; it’s about trusting the Healer. Here's the thing: after a setback, the enemy doesn't just want you to feel bad about what you did—he wants to wreck your identity. He'll whisper, 'You're a fake. You're a failure. You'll never get this right.' That's the shame spiral, and it's his last move to keep you from the Father. But here's the truth: shame is a trap. It's backward-looking, it makes you feel like the problem is who you are, not what you did. Godly sorrow? That's different. It looks forward, it's about fixing your eyes on Jesus, not on your mistakes. So what do you do when the spiral starts? Stop analyzing how bad you feel and start declaring who you are. Say out loud, 'I am a child of God. My sin is covered by the blood. I refuse to listen to the Accuser.' You don't let the enemy define you. You remind your soul the war is already won. You’re loved, accepted, and forgiven—not because you’re perfect, but because Christ is. And that’s not just words—it’s your new reality. When shame tries to drag you back into hiding, you stand up and walk like it. You choose to believe the Father’s promises over the enemy’s lies. It’s like the Prodigal Son story: the dad didn’t make him suffer for his mess—He just wrapped Him in a robe, put a ring on His finger, and threw a party. That’s mercy. It doesn’t downplay the sin, but it magnifies the love. You don’t need to punish yourself to prove you’re sorry, and you don’t get to shrug off sin because grace is 'cheap.' You get to hate the sin more because you see how much it costs the Father to bring you home. So when the shame hits, you don’t hide. You declare your freedom. You walk in the confidence that you’re already loved, and that’s why you choose to be pure. That’s how you break the spiral—for good. Here's the deal about mercy after you mess up: the enemy wants to push you into one of two traps. First, legalism—the voice that says, 'You have to punish yourself. No fun, no joy until you've 'paid' for it.' That’s just shame in disguise, making you your own savior. But then there’s the other trap: license, which says, 'Eh, grace covers it. Do it again next week.' That’s not freedom—it’s letting the cross become cheap. But God’s mercy isn’t either of those. Look at the Prodigal Son story. That kid came back expecting to be a hired hand, thinking he’d lost his identity. But his dad didn’t let him finish his apology speech. No contracts, no performance reviews. The dad gave him a robe, a ring, and a feast. He said, 'You’re still my son.' That’s mercy. It doesn’t minimize the son’s waste—his dad called him ‘dead’ and ‘lost’—but it magnifies the dad’s love. True mercy is this: you’re so grateful for forgiveness that you don’t want to go back to the mud. You start hating sin more because you see what it cost God to bring you home. Grace isn’t a license to keep failing; it’s a fire to keep rising. You don’t ‘have’ to be pure to be loved—you ‘get’ to be pure because you’re already loved. That’s the balance. Legalism keeps you in the shame spiral. License keeps you in the cycle. But mercy? Mercy gives you confidence to change, not permission to stay. It’s the dad’s kiss in the dirt, saying, ‘You’re home. Now let’s move forward.’ That’s the grace we need to walk in.
