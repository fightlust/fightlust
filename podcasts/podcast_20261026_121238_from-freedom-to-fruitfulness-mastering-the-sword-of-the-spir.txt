From Freedom to Fruitfulness: Mastering the Sword of the Spirit for Lasting Impact

This episode unpacks how your personal journey of freedom becomes a weapon for others' liberation. You'll learn to overcome the lie that you must be 'finished' before helping others, discover practical ways to speak truth with grace, and build a chain of discipleship that multiplies God's impact. Whether through casual conversations, structured teaching, or everyday relationships, you'll see how your redeemed history becomes a bridge to someone else's breakthrough.

================================================================

In my book Only Faith, Book 6, Sword of the Spirit, we turn to Chapter 11—From Defense to Mission—and confront a truth that changes everything about how we see our freedom. You’ve spent time fighting your own battles, maybe wondering if your scars are too fresh or your victories too small to matter. But here’s what I want you to hear: your story, the one the enemy tried to ruin, is exactly the message someone else needs. This isn’t about having it all together. It’s about being further down the road than someone else. You don’t need a perfect life to help a broken one. You just need to be willing to turn around, step out of your safety zone, and offer your hand. Sound uncomfortable? That’s because it is. Defense feels safe—your routines, your recovery, your quiet time with God. But the mission asks you to leave that behind, to walk toward someone else’s chaos instead of away from it. And here’s the kicker: the mission field isn’t some distant place. It’s the coworker who’s struggling in silence, the friend who’s isolated, the person in your Legion group who looks exactly like you did two years ago. Your freedom wasn’t the finish line—it was training for this. Today, we’re talking about how to stop letting lies like ‘I’m not ready’ or ‘I’m not qualified’ keep you from being the bridge God wants you to be. Because the truth you carried through your darkest moments? Someone else is desperate for it. Let’s stop hiding our scars and start using them to light the way. Here’s the thing about speaking truth to someone else’s battle: it’s not about dropping wisdom bombs or sounding like you’ve got it all figured out. Real help starts with showing up—really showing up. Ask questions first. Let them talk. Listen longer than you think you should. If someone’s drowning in shame, they don’t need a theology lesson right away. They need to know you’re willing to sit in the mess with them before you try to fix it. Once they feel safe, that’s when you bring truth, but not as a lecture. Pair it with grace. Say something like, ‘You’re not alone in this, and there’s a way forward.’ Truth without grace just adds shame. Grace without truth leaves them stuck. The trick is hitting both at the same time. And here’s a pro tip: use your own story to build a bridge. Don’t go full testimony, though. A quick, honest sentence like, ‘I used to hide in that same shame too’ can open the door. But keep the focus on their fight, not your spotlight. And if the battle’s bigger than you can handle alone? Don’t fake it. Point them to a Legion group, a counselor, or someone else who can help. You’re not failing—you’re being wise. The goal is their freedom, not your ego. So practice asking three questions before you answer. Let your story be a bridge, not the main event. And remember, you’re not their whole rescue team. Sometimes the kindest thing you can do is say, ‘Let’s find you more help.’ That’s how you speak truth without sounding like a know-it-all. It’s presence, plus grace, plus just enough of your own story to show you’ve been there. And when in doubt, keep it simple: listen first, then speak with both kindness and clarity. So here’s the deal—teaching someone to fight spiritually isn’t about grand gestures or fancy words. It’s about showing them the actual tools you’ve used, step by step. Like, if they’re struggling with temptation, you don’t just say, “Have faith,” you walk them through the Bounce Technique—how to pause, name the lie, and swap it with a truth from Scripture. Or if they’re stuck in negative thinking, you teach them the Philippians 4:8 filter, helping them list what’s true, noble, and right in real time. The goal is to make these skills second nature for them, just like they became for you. And you don’t stop at explaining; you practice with them. Maybe you sit side by side, building their 3x5x7 Scripture list together, or role-play using the sword during a tough moment. The key is to model what it looks like to fight daily—not pretending you never struggle, but showing them how you handle it when you do. You’re not trying to be a perfect mentor; you’re being honest about your own journey, which actually makes you more relatable. And here’s the kicker: you’re not just helping one person. You’re training them to pass it on. That’s the whole point of 2 Timothy 2:2—what you received, you give to someone else, and they give it to someone else. So when you teach someone the mechanics of spiritual warfare, you’re not just fixing their problem today. You’re equipping them to fight for themselves tomorrow, and to help someone else the day after that. It’s a chain reaction. And the best part? You don’t have to be an expert to start. You just need to be further down the road than they are, and willing to walk beside them until they’re ready to lead their own fight. Here’s the deal: your job isn’t to be the final teacher in someone’s journey. It’s to pass on what you’ve learned so they can do the same for someone else. Think of it like a chain—what you received, you hand to a friend, and they hand it to another. That’s the power of 2 Timothy 2:2. How do you make it real? Start by asking simple questions early, like, “Who might God want you to share this with next?” It plants the seed of multiplication before they’ve even finished their own fight. And don’t hide your struggles. If you’re wrestling with temptation or doubt, show them what you’re doing to fight it. That’s way more helpful than pretending you’ve got it all figured out. The goal isn’t to keep truth to yourself—it’s to teach someone how to use the tools you’ve been given. Sit with them, practice the skills together, and watch the truth take root. Over time, they’ll stop needing you to hold their hand and start passing it on. That’s how freedom multiplies. It’s not about being perfect. It’s about being honest, being present, and trusting that what God did in you can do the same work in someone else—if you just hand it over. So here’s the thing—using the sword of the Spirit in your daily life means seeing the people around you as mission fields. Your coworkers, your family, your friends—they’re all right there, and you don’t need some big plan to start. Just small, low-pressure moves work. Maybe a text to check in, a coffee invite, or a simple question like, “How are you really doing?” The key is to let your own story—your struggles and how God helped you—be a bridge. If you’ve battled something, sharing that briefly can help someone else feel less alone. But don’t make it all about you. Keep it short and humble, and let their need stay in the spotlight. Listen first. Really listen. People in the middle of a battle need someone to sit with them in the dark before they’ll trust you to speak into the light. And if their fight is bigger than you can handle alone, it’s okay to point them to a group, a counselor, or a pastor. You don’t have to fix everything. The goal is to create safe spaces where they feel heard, accepted, and maybe even start seeing their story through God’s eyes. It’s about being there, step by step, in real life, every day. You don’t have to be perfect or finished in your own journey. Just be willing to take that first small step toward someone else, and let God use your story to light the way for theirs.
