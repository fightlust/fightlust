From Legal Debt to Spiritual Freedom: Mastering the Shoes of the Gospel of Forgiveness

This episode deciphers the biblical concept of forgiveness as a legal debt release, not an emotional obligation. We'll dismantle toxic myths about forgiveness, explore how bitterness fuels addiction, and confront the hardest debt to release: self-forgiveness. Finally, we'll examine when professional help becomes a vital shield in your restoration journey.

================================================================

In my book fightlust, Book 3, Shoes of the Gospel of Peace, we turn to Chapter 17 — Forgiveness as Freedom — where I walk you through the raw, no-flinches truth about what forgiveness really is. Let me be clear: forgiveness isn’t about pretending the hurt didn’t happen, feeling warm fuzzies toward the person who wrecked you, or letting them off the hook. No. In the Bible, forgiveness is a hard-nosed legal transaction. The Greek word aphiēmi means to cancel a debt — like tearing up a contract. When someone sins against you, they owe you. Holding that debt? That’s you chaining yourself to their choices, nursing bitterness, and letting their past control your freedom. Forgiveness is you saying, ‘I’m done waiting for you to pay me back. I’m handing this to God, and I’m walking free.’ It’s not excusing their sin — their actions were wrong, maybe even monstrous — but it *is* refusing to let their mistakes own your future. And here’s the kicker: unforgiveness doesn’t just rot your heart — it fuels addiction. That anger you’re carrying? It’s the same fire that makes you scroll, binge, or numb yourself. But when you cancel that debt, you drain the tank. And then there’s self-forgiveness — the hardest one. You know, when you beat yourself up for years, thinking God’s forgiveness isn’t enough for *you*. Let me tell you: that’s spiritual arrogance. If Christ’s blood is good enough for your sins, it’s good enough for *you*. Forgiveness isn’t weak — it’s a declaration of war on shame. And I’ll walk you through the exact steps to do it, starting right now. But first? Let’s clear the air about what this *isn’t* — and why trying to ‘move on’ without tearing up that ledger just keeps you trapped. We’ll also peek ahead at when you might need more than just this book — like when the wounds run so deep, you need a professional guide to help you navigate the mines. Let’s get into it. Here's the thing about forgiveness—most people get tangled up in confusing it with things it's not. First off, it's not about letting someone off the hook or pretending their sin didn't hurt you. You're not saying, 'What they did was okay,' because it wasn't. Forgiveness means you acknowledge the pain, the real damage they caused, but you refuse to let their sin keep you a prisoner. It’s like ripping up the contract of resentment you’ve been holding onto. Next, you don’t need to erase the memory of what happened. You don’t have to pretend you don’t remember the betrayal or the pain. What changes is how you feel about it. The memory stays, but the fire of anger or the need for revenge goes out. It becomes a scar, not a wound that’s still bleeding. And finally, forgiveness doesn’t always mean getting back into a relationship. Just because you release someone’s debt doesn’t mean you open your door to them again. You might need to keep a boundary for your own safety—like if someone’s abusive or still harmful. The enemy loves to blur these lines because he wants you stuck in bitterness, thinking you’re weak if you forgive or that you’re being unfair to yourself. But clarity is your weapon here. When you know what forgiveness isn’t, you protect your heart from traps. You’re not excusing evil, you’re not forgetting the past, and you’re not throwing open every door. You’re choosing freedom, not obligation. That’s how you clear the confusion and keep moving forward. Here’s the thing: when you hold onto bitterness, it’s like cooking your soul in a pressure cooker. All that built-up anger and pain creates this internal inflammation, and your brain starts screaming for something to numb it. That’s why you reach for screens, food, substances, or whatever gives a quick dopamine hit—it’s not really about the thing itself. It’s about filling the vacuum left by unprocessed hurt. The truth is, a lot of what we call ‘lust’ or ‘compulsion’ is just anger wearing a disguise. You’re not craving the act; you’re craving justice, closure, or the feeling of being heard. The problem is, when you keep that ledger of pain open, you’re fueling the fire instead of putting it out. Forgiveness isn’t about pretending the hurt didn’t happen—it’s about cutting the cord so you stop chasing temporary fixes to quiet a soul that’s crying out for peace. Think about it: how many times have you blown up at your spouse or lashed out at a coworker, only to realize later it wasn’t their fault? That was your old wound acting up. The solution? Stop letting bitterness be your boss. Start asking, ‘Is this urge about what I’m feeling, or what I’ve been holding onto?’ When you tear up that mental ledger, you empty the tank that’s been feeding your cravings. It’s not magic—it’s math. You can’t keep running on a broken system. You need to hit reset, and that starts by letting go of the debt you’ve been carrying. Easy? No. Necessary? Absolutely. Because the only thing that truly satisfies that ache is freedom, not the fix. Here's the thing about forgiving yourself—it's the toughest debt to let go, but it's also the most critical. You might find it easy to forgive others or even believe God has forgiven you, but that voice in your head keeps score, right? The one that whispers, 'You don't get to be okay because of what you did.' That's where the danger is. Refusing to forgive yourself isn't humility; it's actually pride. You’re basically saying your standards for justice are higher than God’s. You’re acting like Jesus’ sacrifice on the cross was good enough for everyone else but not enough for you. That’s not just wrong—it’s a lie that steals your freedom. The truth is, when you carry around shame and self-loathing, you’re not proving anything to God. You’re just keeping yourself trapped in a courtroom where you’re both the judge and the prisoner. But here’s the breakthrough: self-forgiveness isn’t about sweeping your mistakes under the rug. It’s about accepting that Christ already paid your debt. It’s saying, 'If God sees me as clean, then I have no right to call myself dirty.' You’re not letting yourself off easy—you’re stepping into the truth of who you are in Him. And it’s radical. It’s like sealing a wound so it can’t get infected again. So how do you start? Try writing a letter to yourself. Lay out the pain, the choices, the regrets—but then, at the end, write, 'debt cancelled. I am free!' and burn it. Or say it out loud: 'I accept the finished work of the Cross. I am clean.' Replace the voice that says, 'You’re not enough,' with declarations like, 'I am defined by what Christ has done for me, not what was done to me.' It’s not easy, but it’s the only way to stop letting shame run your life. Your soul deserves that kind of freedom. Some folks think asking for professional help means they don’t trust God, but that’s not true. God uses people to do His work—He gives doctors tools to fix bodies, farmers food to feed us, and counselors wisdom to heal broken hearts. If you’ve been through serious trauma, like childhood abuse or family dysfunction, your body and mind might still be stuck in survival mode, even if your spirit wants to move forward. A counselor who specializes in trauma can help you reset your nervous system so you’re not reliving the past every day. If you’re dealing with addiction plus depression, anxiety, or substance use, those issues are connected like puzzle pieces—you can’t fix one without addressing the whole picture. And if you’ve been using the tools we’ve talked about, like the reject method or the 3x5x7 system, but still feel stuck in cycles of relapse, there might be hidden patterns you’re missing. A counselor gives you a safe space to dig deep without fear of judgment, uses science-backed methods to untangle knots in your brain, and offers a fresh perspective you can’t get from friends or family. This isn’t about replacing prayer or accountability—it’s about building a stronger defense. Think of it as adding a shield to your spiritual armor. God’s the healer, but He often works through trained people to get you where you need to go. If your heart’s still hurting and you’re ready to walk free, asking for help is one of the bravest things you can do.
